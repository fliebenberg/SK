# Recruiting and framing interviews

How to ask, what to say before the questions start, and how to handle the awkward moments. The
guides themselves are in this folder — see [README.md](README.md).

---

## The core problem

Every guide holds the product back until its reactions section. That is not secrecy, and you should
not treat it as secrecy. **Say you are building an app. Do not say what it does.**

The moment someone knows your solution, they stop describing their world and start evaluating your
idea — encouraging you, suggesting features, agreeing that things sound useful. All of that is
worthless. What you need is how their Saturday actually goes.

So position at the level of the **problem**, not the solution:

> "I'm building software for schools to run their sport — fixtures, results, that kind of thing.
> Before I build the wrong thing, I want to understand how it actually works for you at the moment."

That is honest, complete, and gives them nothing to react to.

**Be open about the withholding.** It increases trust rather than damaging it:

> "I'll show you what I'm building at the end. I'm deliberately not doing it first — if I describe
> it now you'll start answering about my idea rather than telling me about your job, and your job
> is the useful part."

Nobody objects to this. It reads as rigour.

## What makes people say yes

| Do | Why |
|---|---|
| **Make them the expert.** "You've run fifteen sports days, I've run none." | People give time to be the authority on something they are good at. Far stronger than asking for a favour — favours get deprioritised. |
| **Say you are not selling anything.** "There's no demo, I'm not going to ask you to buy anything." | Removes the main reason to say no. Say it in the first two lines. |
| **Pre-authorise criticism.** "The most useful thing you can do is tell me where this wouldn't work." | Otherwise politeness costs you the interview. Repeat it in the room. |
| **Name the time and stick to it.** "45 minutes." | Vague asks get vague answers. Never say "a few quick questions" for a 45-minute interview. |
| **Offer the findings back.** "I'll send you what I learn across all of these — you might find the comparison useful." | The best non-cash incentive for this audience. Costs you nothing and is genuinely valuable to a head of sport. |
| **Say no preparation is needed.** | Removes the "I'd need to dig out some numbers first" delay that kills scheduling. |

**Avoid:** the word *survey* (implies five minutes and a form), *feedback* (implies you have
something to show), and any version of "would you use an app that…".

## Where to find people

Roughly in order of yield:

1. **Warm introductions** — someone who already knows them asks on your behalf. By far the highest
   conversion. Ask every participant for referrals; it is the last question in every guide.
2. **Your own network's second ring** — the parent at your club who knows the head of sport at
   another school.
3. **Direct approach to a named person.** Find the actual head of sport, not `info@`. School
   websites list them.
4. **At an event.** Go to a sports day. Arrange the interview for later — do not try to interview
   someone while they are running one.

**A caution on warm contacts:** friends are too nice. They will tell you the idea is great. Lean
harder on §4 and §6 of the admin guide with them — concrete stories about the last real event are
much harder to be polite about than opinions.

**Aim for range, not volume.** Five interviews across a big school, a small school, a club, a
well-run organisation and a chaotic one will teach you more than fifteen at schools like the first
one. Deliberately include one organisation with almost no admin capacity.

## Outreach templates

### Cold email to a head of sport

> **Subject: 45 minutes on how you run your sport programme?**
>
> Hi [name],
>
> I'm building software to help schools run their sport — fixtures, results, that sort of thing —
> and I'm trying to understand how it actually works at the moment before I build the wrong thing.
>
> Would you be willing to give me 45 minutes to walk me through how you organise it? I'm not
> selling anything and there's no demo — I genuinely just want to hear how the job works from
> someone who does it. You've run more sports days than I ever will, and that's the part I can't
> get from anywhere else.
>
> No preparation needed. Happy to come to you, or do it over a call — whatever is easiest.
>
> I'm speaking to a number of schools and clubs, and I'm glad to share what I learn across all of
> them if that's of any use to you.
>
> [Your name]

### WhatsApp to a warm contact

> Hi [name] — I'm building an app for schools and clubs to run their sport, and I'm trying to
> understand how people actually do it now before I build anything. Could I pick your brain for
> 45 min? Not selling anything, nothing to show you — just want to hear how it works from someone
> who does it. Happy to come to you.

### Asking for a referral

> Thanks again — that was genuinely useful. One thing: is there anyone else you'd point me at? I'm
> particularly after people whose set-up is *different* from yours — a smaller club, a different
> sport, somewhere less organised. Happy to reach out cold if you'd rather not make the intro.

### Approaching a coach or scorer through the organisation

Go through the admin where you can — it is faster and avoids anyone feeling checked up on. Ask the
admin to introduce you, and be explicit that you are not evaluating anyone:

> "I'd like to talk to one of your coaches and whoever keeps score. Not to check up on them — I
> want to know what the job is like at their end, because it's usually different from how it looks
> from the office."

## The first two minutes in the room

Before question 1.1, cover this. Keep it to ninety seconds.

1. **Thank them and restate the time.** "I've got you for 45 minutes — I'll keep to that."
2. **Frame, at problem level.** "I'm building software for schools to run their sport. I want to
   understand how it works for you today."
3. **Set the shape.** "I'm going to ask you a lot about how things actually work, and I'll ask for
   specific examples rather than general answers — that's deliberate, it's much more useful."
4. **Explain the withholding.** "I'll show you what I'm building at the end rather than now, so I
   don't put ideas in your head."
5. **Pre-authorise criticism.** "There are no right answers, and the most useful thing you can do
   is tell me where something wouldn't work for you."
6. **Ask about recording.** "Do you mind if I record this? It's so I can listen rather than
   scribble. It won't go anywhere outside this project." Have a fallback plan for notes if no.
7. **Then stop talking.** Ask 1.1 and let silence do the work.

## Awkward moments, and what to say

### "So what does it do?" — asked at the start

This will happen, often before you have sat down. Answer briefly and honestly, then redirect:

> "Fixtures, team lists, live scores, results — the admin of running sport. I'll go through it
> properly at the end. If I do it now you'll start telling me what you think of my idea, and I'd
> much rather hear how you do it today. Can I ask you about that first?"

Said warmly, this always works. If they press a second time, show them — a curious participant is
better than a resentful one — but then work harder to bring them back to concrete stories about the
past rather than opinions about your idea.

### They start designing features for you

Helpful people do this. Do not shut it down; mine it for the underlying problem:

> "That's interesting — when did you last need that? Tell me about that time."

The story is worth ten times the feature request.

### They are being very positive

Positive is not evidence. Push:

> "When did you last have that problem?"
> "What would have to be true for that not to work for you?"
> "What's the argument against?"

If they cannot name a downside, you have not got a real answer yet.

### They say "we manage fine"

Common, and often defensive — they may hear the question as criticism of their organisation. Drop
the abstraction and go concrete:

> "That makes sense. Can you walk me through the last one anyway? I mostly want to understand the
> mechanics."

The pain shows up in the story even when it does not show up in the summary.

### It is turning into a sales conversation

They want a price, a launch date, a commitment. Redirect:

> "Honestly, I'm too early for that — that's why I'm doing these. Can I come back to you when
> there's something to look at?"

Then genuinely do. A person who asked for a price is your best early tester.

### They go badly off-topic

Give it a minute — tangents are often where the real material is. If it is genuinely unrelated:

> "That's useful. Can I take you back to [X] for a moment?"

### You are running out of time

Say so and offer the choice. Do not silently overrun:

> "We're at 45 minutes — I'm happy to stop, or carry on for ten if you have it."

## Afterwards

- **Fill in the "After the interview" block within the hour.** Every guide ends with one. Memory
  degrades fast and the recording will not save you — you will not relisten to all of it.
- **Send a thank-you the same day**, and deliver anything you promised.
- **Ask for the referral** if you did not get one in the room.
- **Revise the guide.** If a question landed badly three times, change it. Add new questions at the
  *end* of a section so existing references stay valid — see [README.md](README.md).
- **Stop when you stop being surprised.** If three interviews in a row tell you nothing new, that
  persona is done. Move to a different kind of organisation rather than more of the same.
